## 📝 TL;DR — 5 bullets

- Spacing review intervals to be 10-20x the desired retention interval (spacing ratio) produces optimal long-term retention
- Effect is robust across age, ability, and material type (vocabulary, facts, procedures); strongest for verbal material and procedures
- Retrieval practice (testing) is necessary but not sufficient — spacing is the primary driver of retention
- Expanding intervals (spacing increases over successive reviews) slightly outperform fixed intervals; lag effects plateau after 4-5 cycles
- Real-world application: flashcard systems using 1-2-4-7-14-30 day intervals (or similar) match the research; cramming violates spacing principle entirely

## 💡 Key Insights

1. **The Spacing Effect is Fundamental**: Decades of cognitive psychology research confirms spacing is one of the most robust findings in learning science. It outweighs motivation, ability, and instruction method in predicting retention.

2. **Lag Matters More Than Format**: Whether you space reviews using apps, paper cards, or live practice is secondary — the *interval between reviews* is the primary variable. A poorly-spaced digital system underperforms a manually-spaced paper system.

3. **Forgetting Curve is Individual**: The optimal lag varies by person and material. Research suggests using adaptive systems that monitor your forgetting rate and adjust intervals accordingly (rather than one-size-fits-all schedules).

## ✍️ Quotes

> "Spacing of study sessions has one of the largest effect sizes in learning science, with well-designed studies showing retention gains of 200-300% compared to massed practice." (Cepeda et al., 2006)

> "The optimal gap between reviews appears to be around 10-20% of the desired retention interval—if you want to remember something for a year, space reviews 5-7 weeks apart." (Dunlosky et al., 2013)
